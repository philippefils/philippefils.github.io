ANALOG CIRCUIT DESIGN

BJT-Based Operational Amplifier

Design, LTspice simulation, stability compensation, and evaluation of a discrete three-stage operational amplifier using commercially available bipolar junction transistors.

Analog Electronics BJT LTspice Feedback Circuit Simulation

OVERVIEW

Building an operational amplifier from discrete transistors

This project involved designing, simulating, and validating a general-purpose operational amplifier constructed from commercially available bipolar junction transistors (BJTs). The work combined amplifier stages developed in previous electronics laboratories into a complete three-stage design.

The amplifier integrated a differential gain stage, a buffer stage, and a Class AB output stage. LTspice was used to evaluate voltage gain, input resistance, frequency response, voltage transfer characteristics, and input and output operating ranges.

A major part of the design process was addressing the stability of the combined amplifier. Phase-margin analysis identified the need for frequency compensation, and alternative capacitor configurations were evaluated before selecting a compensation approach.

AMPLIFIER ARCHITECTURE

Three stages with distinct functions

The amplifier combined three circuit blocks into a single signal chain. Each stage contributed a different function to the overall design.

01 — Differential amplifier

The input stage provides differential signal amplification and uses an active load as part of the voltage-gain architecture.

02 — Buffer stage

A buffer incorporating diode-connected transistors interfaces the differential stage with the common-emitter amplifier stage.

03 — Class AB output stage

The output stage provides the final output drive. The complete amplifier was evaluated with a 330 Ω load.

STAGE 01

Differential input stage

The differential amplifier formed the input stage of the operational amplifier. An active load was incorporated into the stage before integrating it with the buffer and Class AB output circuitry.

STAGE 02

Buffer and common-emitter amplifier

The intermediate stage used diode-connected transistors in the buffer circuit alongside a common-emitter amplifier. This stage was integrated between the differential input stage and the Class AB output stage.

STAGE 03

Class AB output stage

The Class AB output stage completed the amplifier architecture. After combining the three stages, the circuit was evaluated under a 330 Ω load to assess the effects of loading on its gain, frequency response, and operating range.

LTSPICE ANALYSIS

Simulated performance before compensation

LTspice simulations were used to characterize the integrated amplifier before adding frequency compensation. The initial circuit achieved high open-loop gain, but its phase-margin analysis revealed a stability problem.

OPEN-LOOP VOLTAGE GAIN

−2505.29 V/V

Simulated low-frequency voltage gain of the uncompensated amplifier.

INPUT RESISTANCE

886.4 kΩ

Input resistance reported by the initial LTspice analysis.

INITIAL CUTOFF FREQUENCY

9.5 kHz

Reported cutoff frequency before compensation.

INITIAL PHASE MARGIN

−28.73°

Negative phase margin indicated that the uncompensated design did not meet the intended stability requirement.

The initial voltage-transfer analysis also showed that the output remained close to 0 V over an input range of approximately −3.4 V to +3.8 V under the simulated conditions. The report recorded a maximum output swing of 6.07 V and a corresponding maximum input swing of 2.28 mV.

FREQUENCY COMPENSATION

Trading bandwidth for stability

The initial phase-margin result motivated the addition of frequency compensation. Two capacitor configurations were evaluated in LTspice to slow the amplifier's frequency response and improve phase margin.

Option A — 100 nF

A 100 nF capacitor connected between the amplifier input and output.

Phase margin: 42.23°

Cutoff frequency: 117.86 Hz

Option B — 500 pF

A 500 pF capacitor connected between the buffer-stage input node and ground.

Phase margin: 38.34°

Cutoff frequency: 348.51 Hz

Both configurations improved phase margin beyond the project's minimum target of 25°. The 100 nF input-to-output capacitor was selected because it provided the required phase margin using a single compensation capacitor and was considered easier to implement.

The results also demonstrated the trade-off between stability and bandwidth: improving phase margin substantially reduced the amplifier's cutoff frequency.

LOADED PERFORMANCE

Evaluating the amplifier with a 330 Ω load

After selecting the compensation approach, the amplifier was evaluated with a 330 Ω load. The reported voltage gain remained approximately −2505 V/V, indicating that the load had negligible effect on the simulated gain.

LOADED VOLTAGE GAIN

≈ −2505 V/V

Gain remained approximately unchanged after connecting the 330 Ω load.

LOADED CUTOFF FREQUENCY

116.85 Hz

Reported −3 dB cutoff frequency after loading.

LOADED PHASE MARGIN

42.23°

The reported phase margin remained unchanged after connecting the load.

MAXIMUM OUTPUT SWING

6.5 V

Reported maximum output swing, corresponding to a maximum input swing of 2.16 mV.

The loaded voltage-transfer characteristic also remained similar to the initial result, with the output bias point staying near 0 V over the reported input range of −3.4 V to +3.8 V.

Overall, the simulations indicated that the selected compensation configuration met the phase-margin target while maintaining the reported gain under the specified resistive load.

PROTOTYPING & VALIDATION

From simulation to a discrete prototype

The project included a discrete prototype built using commercially available BJT transistors. The overall objective was to design, simulate, and validate the operation of a general-purpose operational amplifier.

The analysis documented here focuses on the report's LTspice results, including gain, input resistance, frequency response, voltage-transfer characteristics, operating ranges, and compensation. These simulation results are distinguished from measured hardware performance.

FUTURE IMPROVEMENTS

Potential next steps

  • Compare simulated and measured gain, bandwidth, and phase margin on the physical prototype.
  • Investigate the bandwidth reduction introduced by the selected compensation capacitor.
  • Evaluate the effects of transistor variation and parasitic capacitances on stability.
  • Further characterize distortion and output behaviour across different load conditions.
