MIXED-SIGNAL HARDWARE

Multichannel SiPM Scintillation Detector

Electrical hardware design for a modular scintillation detector developed for automated PET radiopharmaceutical synthesis.

Analog PCB Design ESP32 Signal Processing

PROJECT OVERVIEW

Modular radiation detection hardware

As part of my electrical engineering capstone project, I designed the electrical hardware for a modular scintillation detector in collaboration with the Montreal Neurological Institute. The system uses silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) to detect radiation-induced scintillation and process the resulting electrical pulses before digitization by an ESP32.

Each detector module provides two independent detection channels. Two modules can be stacked to create a four-channel detector system. The modules share power through a dedicated interconnection while remaining independently removable, allowing the system to scale without redesigning the electronics.

My work focused on the electrical hardware, including the analog signal-processing circuitry, SiPM interface, power circuitry, schematic capture, PCB layout, assembly, and hardware validation.

SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

Two-channel modular detector

The modular architecture was designed around scalability and maintainability. Each module contains its own signal-processing circuitry, SiPM interface, power circuitry, and microcontroller interface. Power can be supplied to one module and distributed through the stack, eliminating the need for separate power connections for every module.

ANALOG SIGNAL PROCESSING

SiPM signal conditioning

Each channel uses a MICROFC-60035-SMT-TR SiPM operated at approximately 29.5 V bias. The raw SiPM pulse is first amplified using an OPA354 in a non-inverting configuration before entering a dedicated peak-detection stage.

The peak detector uses two OPA354 amplifiers, a BSS138 NMOS transistor, and a BAT54S Schottky diode. The circuit captures the amplitude of short SiPM pulses and holds the detected peak long enough for the ESP32 ADC to sample it.

POWER DESIGN

SiPM bias and board power

The detector accepts a 5 V external supply through a DC barrel connector. The board distributes the 5 V supply to the required circuitry while the SiPM bias voltage is generated locally using a DC-DC boost converter.

The boost stage generates approximately 29.5 V required to bias the SiPM. The remaining circuitry operates from the available 5 V and 3.3 V rails, with local supply decoupling used throughout the design.

PCB DESIGN

From schematic to assembled hardware

I designed the detector PCB in Altium Designer, integrating the SiPM interface, analog signal-processing circuitry, power electronics, microcontroller connections, and dedicated test points into a compact two-layer board.

The analog circuitry was physically clustered together and signal paths were kept short and direct to reduce unnecessary noise coupling. Test points were incorporated throughout the signal chain to allow individual stages to be probed during validation.

HARDWARE VALIDATION

Assembly, debugging, and testing

The PCBs were manufactured by JLCPCB and I performed the board assembly using both reflow and hand soldering techniques. Hardware validation was performed progressively, testing individual portions of the board before full system integration.

Initial continuity testing was used to verify solder joints, identify opens, and check for unintended shorts. The SiPM interface was then independently tested before powering the board.

The amplifier and peak-detection stages were validated using a function generator to inject known signals while monitoring the outputs with an oscilloscope. This allowed the gain, signal response, peak detection, and hold behavior to be evaluated independently.

FUTURE IMPROVEMENTS

Future improvements

Future iterations of the detector could incorporate additional analog filtering to further reduce unwanted noise and improve the quality of the signal presented to the ADC.

The grounding and power distribution could also be refined by improving the separation between the analog and digital portions of the system. The prototype used a common ground, while a more deliberate grounding strategy could help reduce noise coupling between the different sections of the board.

The modular architecture, replaceable interfaces, and accessible test points also provide a foundation for further development, troubleshooting, and expansion of the detector system.
