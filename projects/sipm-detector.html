MIXED-SIGNAL HARDWARE

Multichannel SiPM Scintillation Detector

A cost-effective, modular scintillation detector designed for automated PET radiopharmaceutical synthesis.

PROJECT TYPE Engineering Capstone
APPLICATION Medical Instrumentation
HARDWARE SiPM · ESP32 · Analog Front-End
TOOLS Altium Designer · LTSpice

01 — OVERVIEW

Project Overview

In collaboration with the Montreal Neurological Institute, my team and I developed a two-channel scintillation detector for monitoring radioactivity during the automated synthesis of positron emission tomography (PET) radiopharmaceuticals.

The detector uses silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) to convert detected scintillation photons into electrical pulses. These pulses are amplified and processed through a peak-detection stage before being sampled by an ESP32 ADC and transmitted to a PC-based interface.

The objective was to develop a cost-effective, modular, and scalable detector that could be integrated into an automated radiopharmaceutical synthesis system.

02 — SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

Signal Chain

The detector's signal path was designed around three main hardware stages:

01 — SiPM

MICROFC-60035-SMT-TR silicon photomultiplier operated at approximately 29.5 V bias.

02 — Amplification

OPA354 non-inverting amplifier used to condition the SiPM output signal.

03 — Peak Detection

OPA354-based peak detection circuit converts the short detector pulse into a measurable voltage.

04 — Data Acquisition

ESP32 ADC samples the processed signal for measurement and analysis.

The complete signal path is: SiPM → Amplification → Peak Detection → ESP32 ADC.

The resulting measurement data is transmitted to a PC-based interface for monitoring and analysis.

03 — ANALOG FRONT-END

Signal Amplification & Peak Detection

Signal Amplification

The SiPM produces relatively small electrical pulses that require amplification before being sampled by the microcontroller.

The amplification stage uses an OPA354 in a non-inverting configuration. The SiPM signal is connected to the non-inverting input through a series resistor, while the feedback network determines the amplifier response and gain.

Peak Detection

Following amplification, the signal passes through a peak-detection circuit built around two OPA354 amplifiers, an NMOS transistor, and a BAT54S Schottky diode.

The circuit captures the peak value of the detector pulse, converting the short-duration SiPM signal into a voltage that can be sampled reliably by the ESP32 ADC.

04 — POWER & PCB DESIGN

Power Architecture

The board is powered from a 5 V barrel-jack input and generates the approximately 29.5 V SiPM bias using a boost converter.

The system also uses the 3.3 V supply associated with the ESP32 for the low-voltage digital circuitry. Local VDD decoupling capacitors were used throughout the design to support stable operation of the active devices.

PCB Development

The detector was implemented on a two-layer PCB designed in Altium Designer.

The analog circuitry was physically clustered together, with sensitive signal paths kept as short and direct as practical. This approach helped minimize unnecessary trace length and potential coupling into the low-level detector signals.

The PCB was manufactured by JLCPCB and subsequently assembled by me using a combination of reflow soldering and hand soldering.

The prototype used a common ground architecture. A future revision would provide an opportunity to further evaluate analog/digital grounding and power distribution strategies.

05 — EMBEDDED DATA ACQUISITION

System Integration

06 — TESTING & VALIDATION

Hardware Validation

The analog circuitry was tested independently before integration into the complete system.

A function generator was used to provide representative input signals to the analog front-end, while an oscilloscope was used to observe and characterize the resulting signals at different stages of the circuit.

The completed detector was subsequently tested with the SiPM to capture actual detector pulses.

Additional electrical measurements were performed using a multimeter during hardware bring-up and debugging.

07 — ENGINEERING LESSONS

Design Improvements

The project provided practical experience designing and integrating a complete mixed-signal measurement system, while also identifying areas where the design could be improved in a future revision.

In particular, additional filtering of the analog signal path would be considered in a subsequent revision to improve noise rejection and signal conditioning.

The grounding and analog/digital power architecture could also be refined further. The prototype used a common ground architecture, and a future revision would provide an opportunity to evaluate improved analog/digital isolation and grounding strategies.

These lessons reinforced the importance of considering signal integrity, noise, grounding, and power distribution early in the design of low-level mixed-signal hardware.
